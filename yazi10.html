"Nothing is perfect. Life is messy. Relationships are complex. Outcomes are uncertain."

Last night as I was surfing Pinterest, I saw and saved a quote on my board: "Nothing is perfect. Life is messy. Relationships are complex. Outcomes are uncertain."
I have an amazing, supportive, loving family. My three younger siblings, parents, and I live a pretty joyful life. However, as the quote says, relationships are complex, and outcomes are uncertain. In this writing, I want to talk about the waves I experienced through my childhood that resulted in who I am today and made me grow into this version of myself.
My mother was probably just out of the hospital from giving birth to my youngest brother, and my father was in another city for work. The 9-year-old me crying myself to sleep because my parents missed my end-of-year portfolio presentation wouldn't have guessed that those tears eventually made me stronger. I remember the days when I wrote down my wishes on little pieces of paper and dug them under a rosewood: "Dear God, please don't let my dad miss my birthday," which he missed because of work. Seeing my mother deal with the four of us by herself was difficult. I cannot lie; she was, and is excellent at taking care of four little kids alone.
She usually fell asleep on the living room couch while feeding the newborn baby. I remember that I walked on the balls of my feet to check if she was awake and if she could come to my room for a short mom and daughter time :)
She was rarely sleeping through the night, which made me feel like I had to do more to help her with the responsibility of raising us since I was the oldest kid. Each night, I would wait until she fell asleep, and through the night, when one of my brothers woke up, I would run to their room and try to make them sleep again so that my mom wouldn't wake up from their crying. Being a mother is a unique and divine blessing. I am not a mother and am too young to be one. Yet, I feel like I have experienced some bits of being a mother from taking care of my younger siblings as they grew. I felt the responsibility and the mothering instinct to protect them from even the tiniest things.
One weekend, my father was back home. My second youngest brother was six years old then. He wanted to eat his third ice cream that day, and expectedly, my mom didn't let him. Just like a six-year-old kid would do, he started screaming and throwing some of his toys around. I learned how to calm him down, what he enjoyed, what distracted him, how he acted when angry or upset, and what facial expression he made when he wanted something. He would be in peace simply if I handed him his favorite toy, a squishy dinosaur. I knew him because I was there all those six years. However, my father was not. He didn't know how to act against him screaming around, and he started yelling back at him.
I was beginning to feel the heat. That was not how you should deal with these types of crying crises. As an 11-year-old kid, I warned him: "This only makes things worse. He is not learning; he is scared of you. He will never forget this day and your behavior when he gets older." Because that was what happened to me. The response I got from him was not satisfying. "Stay out of this and go to your room. Yes, he will be scared and always remember this day and so will not act like this again. I am teaching him not to now. He needs to learn not to scream". In fact, he was trying to teach him not to yell by yelling at him. His statement was completely wrong. My brother was terrified and still is to this day. As soon as my father went back to the other city for work, my mom and I were the ones dealing with the damaging outcomes of my dad's wrong attitude towards my siblings. I read numerous books on child development, watched many TV shows like supernanny, and created methods of fixing my siblings' behavior.
My brothers LOVED stickers. They also LOVED cursing. Thus, to solve this, I got two tiny notebooks, customized the covers by writing their names on each, and drawing little star and heart symbols. Whenever they felt like cursing, they would realize it, replace the curse with another word, and win a sticker to add to their sticker notebook. Looking back, the 5th grade Ece was brilliant in using positive reinforcement to teach them not to curse.
My siblings have hugely impacted me and my life without being aware of it. Four months from now, I will be in college studying Cognitive Science and hopefully working in a child development lab.
So, nothing is perfect, life is messy, relationships are complex, and outcomes are uncertain. Ten years ago, as I was looking after my brothers, I wouldn't believe it if someone told me that those days would form who I am today this much. Little Ece would be proud :)
